- For simple distributions, the derivative of the log-likelihood can be computed easily, making MLE straightforward. - However, for more complex ones, we can encounter difficulties: - There may be latent (hidden) variables that are not observed in the data. - The log-likelihood may involve a log of a sum, which is not straightforward to optimize. --- ### Core idea of EM - The key idea of EM is the introduction of hidden variables to simplify the optimization problem. - The introduced hidden variable $Y$, if known, would make the likelihood easier to optimize. - We now have a .nord14[complete-data log-likelihood] $\\log (L(\\Theta | X, Y))$ --- ### EM algorithm EM is an iterative algorithm that alternates between two steps: - **E-step (Expectation step)**: Compute the expected value of the log-likelihood with respect to the hidden variables, given the current parameter estimates. .open-color-red-7[ $$ Q(\\Theta | \\Theta^{(i-1)}) = \\mathbb{E}[\\log (p(X, Y | \\Theta)) | X, \\Theta^{(i-1)}] $$ ] - **M-step (Maximization step)**: Maximize this expected log-likelihood to update the parameter estimates. .open-color-green-7[ $$ \\Theta^{(i)} = \\underset{\\Theta}{\\operatorname{argmax}} Q(\\Theta | \\Theta^{(i-1)}) $$ ] **Intuition**: We optimize a proxy that keeps improving the real objective; the likelihood curve increases step by step. --- class: nord-light, center, middle # Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) .letter-spacing-20[first application] --- ### Definition One of the most common applications of EM is the mixture-density estimation problem because with the probabilistic model $p(x | \\Theta) = \\sum_{i=1}^M \\alpha_i p_i(x | \\theta_i)$ , we face a .nord11[log of a sum] in the log-likelihood. However, if we introduce a hidden variable $Y$ .nord14[that indicates which component generated each data point], the complete-data log-likelihood becomes easier to optimize. And then if the component distributions are Gaussian, it is possible to get the analytical expressions of the parameters. --- ### E-step for GMM : soft clustering This step computes the "responsibilities", which are the posterior probabilities of each component $l$ using Bayes' rule. $$ p(l | x\\_i, \\Theta^g) = \\frac{\\alpha^g\\_l p\\_l(x\\_i | \\theta^g\\_l)}{\\sum\\_{j=1}^M \\alpha^g\\_j p\\_j(x\\_i | \\theta^g\\_j)} $$ Each data point is softly assigned to every component with a certain probability and the $Q$ function is: $$ Q(\\Theta | \\Theta^{g}) = \\sum\\_{l=1}^M \\sum\\_{i=1}^N \\log(\\alpha\\_l p\\_l(x\\_i | \\theta\\_l)) p(l | x\\_i, \\Theta^g) $$ --- ### M-step for GMM : weighted update - $\\alpha^{new}_l$ : average responsibility (i.e., expected proportion of points assigned to component l) $$ \\alpha^{new}\\_l = \\frac{1}{N} \\sum\\_{i=1}^N p(l | x\\_i, \\Theta^g) $$ - $\\mu^{new}_l$ : weighted average of all data points, weighted by responsibility $$ \\mu^{new}\\_l = \\frac{\\sum\\_{i=1}^N x\\_i p(l | x\\_i, \\Theta^g)}{\\sum\\_{i=1}^N p(l | x\\_i, \\Theta^g)} $$ - $\\Sigma^{new}_l$ : responsibility-weighted covariance $$ \\Sigma^{new}\\_l = \\frac{\\sum\\_{i=1}^N p(l | x\\_i, \\Theta^g) (x\\_i - \\mu^{new}\\_l)(x\\_i - \\mu^{new}\\_l)^T}{\\sum\\_{i=1}^N p(l | x\\_i, \\Theta^g)} $$ --- class: nord-light, center, middle # Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) .letter-spacing-20[second application] --- ### Definition A Hidden Markov Model is a probabilistic model of the joint probability of a collection of random variables: $ \\lbrace O\\_1, \\ldots, O\\_T, Q\\_1, \\ldots, Q\\_T \\rbrace $ . $O\\_t$ are continuous/discrete observations and $Q\\_t$ are the discrete hidden states producing the observations. We assume an initial state distribution $\\pi$, and the fact that the underlying Markov chain is time-homogeneous so we have transition matrix $A = \\lbrace a\_{ij} \\rbrace$ and the emission probabilities $B = \\lbrace b\\_j(o\\_t) \\rbrace$. The model is denoted by $ \\lambda = (\\pi, A, B) $ .block-middle.width-50[ .mermaid[

] ] --- ### The forward-backward trick Computing $P(Y|X, \\Theta)$ in this case means summing over all $N^T$ possible state sequences, this is exponential, the forward-backward algorithm reduces this to $O(N^2T)$. The trick is to isolate the probability of being in a state $i$ at time $t$ by intersecting the past (forward) and the future (backward). $$ \\alpha\\_i(t) \\beta\\_i(t) = p(O, Q\\_t = i | \\lambda) $$ --- ### E-step for HMM - $ \\gamma\\_i(t) = p(Q\\_t = i | O, \\lambda) $ : probability of being in state $i$ at time $t$: $$ \\gamma\\_i(t) = \\frac{\\alpha\\_i(t) \\beta\\_i(t)}{\\sum\\_{j=1}^N \\alpha\\_j(t) \\beta\\_j(t)} $$ - $ \\xi\\_{ij}(t) = p(Q\\_t = i, Q\\_{t+1} = j | O, \\lambda) $ : probability of transitioning from state $i$ to state $j$ at time $t$: $$ \\xi\\_{ij}(t) = \\frac{\\alpha\\_i(t) a\\_{ij} b\\_j(o\\_{t+1}) \\beta\\_j(t+1)}{\\sum\\_{k=1}^N \\sum\\_{l=1}^N \\alpha\\_k(t) a\\_{kl} b\\_l(O\\_{t+1}) \\beta\\_l(t+1)} $$ --- ### M-step for HMM : Update - Initial state distribution: $$ \\pi\\_i^{new} = \\gamma\\_i(1) $$ - Transition probabilities: $$ a\\_{ij}^{new} = \\frac{\\sum\\_{t=1}^{T-1} \\xi\\_{ij}(t)}{\\sum\\_{t=1}^{T-1} \\gamma\\_i(t)} $$ - Emission probabilities: $$ b\\_j^{new}(k) = \\frac{\\sum\\_{t=1}^T \\delta\\_{o\\_t,v\\_k} \\gamma\\_j(t)}{\\sum\\_{t=1}^T \\gamma\\_j(t)} $$ The same results can be obtained by estimating using the $Q$ function. --- ### Conclusion - EM is a powerful algorithm to solve MLE problems with latent variables and complex likelihoods. - Each iteration of EM guarantees an increase in the likelihood, but it may converge to a local maximum. - The E-step replaces unknowns with expectations - The M-step is just weighted maximum likelihood
